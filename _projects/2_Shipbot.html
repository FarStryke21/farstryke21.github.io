Project site

Cargo vessels need routine checks — valves, gauges, breakers — carried out in cramped, corroded, moving spaces that are unpleasant to send a person into and unpleasant for hardware to survive. BlackFlag was our answer to that brief: a utility robot built over a semester-long mechatronics capstone, with enough dexterity to operate ship fittings and enough robustness to keep working in a marine environment.

The BlackFlag marine vessel support robot.

The system pairs a 5-degree-of-freedom revolute arm with a mobile base and a custom perception package. Five degrees of freedom is the interesting constraint: it is one short of being able to reach an arbitrary pose, so the arm has to be positioned by the base rather than solving for reach on its own — which makes navigation and manipulation a single coupled problem instead of two separate ones.

My role

I led the perception and sensing subsystem across the five-person team, and took on software integration and the final deployment of the working system. Perception is what tells the arm where a valve actually is, as opposed to where the model says it should be — the gap that decides whether a manipulation attempt succeeds or scrapes past.

Full build details, subsystem breakdowns and demonstration footage are on the team's project site.
